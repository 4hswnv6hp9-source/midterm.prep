ICQ Instruction Checking Question

Checks that students know what to DO in a task, not what words mean.

  • Ask about grouping, task, time, output and materials.
  • Ask after the instructions, before students start.
  • Avoid “Do you understand?” A “yes” does not show understanding.
Instruction: “In pairs, choose three pictures. You have two minutes.”
ICQs: “Alone or in pairs?” “How many pictures?” “How long?”
Practice. Instruction: “Listen twice. First, choose the best title individually; then compare with a partner.”
Good ICQs: “How many times will you listen?” “Do you choose alone first or with your partner?”
Not an ICQ: “What does ‘title’ mean?” That checks vocabulary, not the task.
CCQ Concept Checking Question

Checks that students understand the meaning of a word, phrase or grammar.

  • Keep questions short, clear and simpler than the target language.
  • Do not repeat the target expression in the question.
  • Check meaning, not only form.
  • Always give a context. Meaning can change with context.
Target: “She used to live in Samarkand.”
“Did she live there in the past?” (Yes)
“Does she live there now?” (No, in the usual meaning. Context needed.)
Target: “He must be at home.” (His lights are on and his car is outside.)
“Am I completely certain?” (No)
“Do I think it is very likely?” (Yes)
Not a CCQ for meaning: “Is must a modal verb?” That is a form question.
ICQ vs CCQ quick comparison
ICQ
Checks the task procedure.
“Do you write or speak?”
Comes after task instructions, before students start.
CCQ
Checks language meaning.
“Is the action finished?”
Comes after presenting or clarifying target language.

Both need observable evidence of understanding. “Is it clear?” is not enough.

Schemata prior knowledge

The organised knowledge and expectations students bring to a text or topic.

  • Content schemata: what students already know about the subject.
  • Formal schemata: what they expect from the text type and its organisation (interview, news report).
  • Before listening: show a photo, discuss the topic, predict ideas.
Careful: activating schemata supports understanding. It is not teaching every answer in advance.
Eliciting draw it out

Prompting students to supply language, ideas or knowledge instead of giving it straight away.

  • Techniques: pictures, mime, situations, examples, prompts, guided questions.
  • Use it when students can reasonably find the answer. If the knowledge is missing, stop guessing and tell them.
  • Benefits: participation, diagnostic feedback, links to prior knowledge.
Show a person carrying an umbrella under dark clouds. Ask “What might happen?” Students say “It might rain.”
Listening lesson typical sequence
  1. Lead-in / context. Create interest and activate relevant schemata.
  2. Pre-teach blocking vocabulary. Only if essential, not every unknown word.
  3. Gist task. Listen for overall meaning. Set the task before playing the audio.
  4. Detail task. Listen again for specific information.
  5. Feedback. Pair-check, then confirm answers and evidence.
  6. Follow-up. Respond to the content or use language from the text.
Memory trick: Little Pigs Go Dancing, Feeling Fabulous = Lead-in, Pre-teach, Gist, Detail, Feedback, Follow-up.

Lesson sequences are flexible, not an inflexible CELTA formula.

One-minute revision self-check
  • ICQ = “What do I do?” CCQ = “What does it mean?”
  • Schemata = prior knowledge and expectations.
  • Eliciting = drawing out what students already know.
  • Listening = context, purposeful first listen, focused second listen, feedback, response.
  • Exam strategy: define the term, give a realistic example, explain why it works.
